Moving people is part of almost every shift: helping someone stand, turning them in bed, getting into a wheelchair or a car. Done wrong, it hurts the person and it hurts you. In this lesson you learn to check the equipment, follow the plan, move people safely step by step, and protect their skin from pressure injuries.

1 Manual handling: before you move anyone

Manual handling of people means any job where you use force to lift, hold, push, pull, move, support or lower a person. The Queensland code of practice says: no worker should fully lift a person on their own (except a small baby). Use equipment, aids, or another worker.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S1 P1Manual handling injuries are common, and most of them can be prevented by following your organisation's policies and procedures.
  2. NP9 S1 P2Risks for the person: being dropped or falling, being hurt by equipment used the wrong way, being injured by poor technique.
  3. NP9 S1 P3Risks for you: not following procedures, not using equipment properly, not clearing the area, taking shortcuts to save time.
  4. NP9 S1 P4Check the equipment before you use it: it must be clean, and in good condition (no cracks, loose parts, tears or damage that could hurt someone). If it is broken or dirty, do not use it: tag it and report it.
  5. NP9 S1 P5Put the equipment in the right place: where you and the person can reach it easily when you need it.
  6. NP9 S1 P6Check it suits the person (the right size, sling, height, settings) and that you are trained to use it.
  7. NP9 S1 P7Ask first: explain what you are going to do and get the person's consent. Encourage them to do as much as they can themselves.
  8. NP9 S1 P8Look at the environment: where are you moving from and to? Clear anything in the way before you start.
  9. NP9 S1 P9Protect your back: bend your knees, keep your back straight, do not twist, tighten your tummy muscles, and get help if it is too heavy.

2 Equipment and aids for moving people

Different equipment does different jobs. The individual plan says which equipment this person uses. Flip through the cards to learn what each one looks like.

Listen to the explanation

Slide sheets

Slippery sheets that help you turn or move a person in bed without lifting and without rubbing their skin.

Pat slide (slide board)

A smooth board to slide a person from one flat surface to another, for example bed to trolley.

Monkey bar (trapeze)

A handle over the bed. The person pulls on it to help move themselves.

Bed rails

Rails on the side of the bed. They can help a person turn, but they can also be a restraint: only use them as the plan says.

Walk belt (transfer belt)

A belt around the person's waist with handles, so you can guide and steady them.

Walking stick

For a person who needs a little support and balance.

Walking frame (hopper frame)

A frame with no wheels. The person lifts it forward, then steps. Gives a lot of stability.

Wheelie walker and rollator

A frame with wheels and brakes. Some have a seat. The person pushes it as they walk.

Wheelchair

Pushed by a worker or by the person. Brakes, foot plates and arm rests must be checked and set.

Motorised wheelchair and scooter

Driven by the person. Juan uses a motorised wheelchair.

Standing aid (Sara Stedy)

The person pulls up to stand and sits on the flip-down seats while you wheel them a short distance.

Weight bearing (standing) hoist

For a person who can take some weight on their legs. A sling goes around their back. Juan uses this one.

Non-weight bearing hoist

For a person who cannot take weight on their legs. A full body sling lifts them completely. Used after a fall.

Ceiling hoist

A hoist on a track in the ceiling. It does the same job, with no hoist base to move around.

3 Slide sheets: why, and who pulls which layer

Your assessment gives you four reasons and asks you to pick the two correct ones. Learn them in the same words.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S3 P1Correct: "Reduces the risk of friction and shearing injuries on the person." The sheets slide on each other, so the person's skin is not dragged.
  2. NP9 S3 P2Correct: "Reduces injuries to support workers." You slide instead of lift.
  3. NP9 S3 P3Not a reason: "It is quicker, saving time of the support workers." (Safety comes first, not speed.)
  4. NP9 S3 P4Not a reason: "Enables an easier lift to be executed." (Slide sheets are for sliding, not lifting.)
  5. NP9 S3 P5Who pulls which layer? Two slide sheets are under the person. To roll the person onto their right side, the worker on the person's right side (the side they are rolling towards) pulls the top layer towards themselves. The other worker supports and guides. In the assessment picture this is Staff member B pulls on top layer.

4 Helping a person stand up: the 9 instructions

You do not pull the person up. You prompt them, one step at a time, so they use their own strength. Your assessment asks you to put the 9 instructions in order. Learn them in this order.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S4 P1Uncross legs
  2. NP9 S4 P2Wriggle bottom forward in the chair so that you are sitting towards the front of the cushion, and feet are on the floor
  3. NP9 S4 P3Feet on floor, comfortable distance, approximately hip width apart
  4. NP9 S4 P4Place hands on arm (or seat) of chair
  5. NP9 S4 P5Use a rocking motion – Forward – Back (1)
  6. NP9 S4 P6Forward – Back (2)
  7. NP9 S4 P7Rocking forward (3), nose towards knees, push forward with hands
  8. NP9 S4 P8Use leg muscles to straighten legs, which raises your centre of gravity off chair
  9. NP9 S4 P9Straighten back coming to a standing position

5 Bed to wheelchair: set it up, mind the parts that hurt

Lez can stand with a little help, but needs a wheelchair to get around. Your assessment asks how to position the wheelchair, and which parts of a wheelchair can hurt a person during a transfer.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S5 P1Where the wheelchair goes: close to the bed and flush along the side of the bed (parallel to it).
  2. NP9 S5 P2Foot plates raised and swung back out of the way (retracted).
  3. NP9 S5 P3Brakes ON before the person moves.
  4. NP9 S5 P4Parts that can hurt the person: the foot plates, the legs (leg rests), the connections for the legs, and the sides (arm rests and frame). A shin or ankle can hit or scrape them.
  5. NP9 S5 P5After the transfer: put the foot plates back down, help the person place their feet, then take the brakes off.

A wheelchair ready for a transfer

Check the brakes, foot plates and arm rests before you start.

Foot plates swung away

Foot plates up and out of the way, so the person cannot trip on them or hit their legs.

The sides and leg connections

Arm rests, sides and the leg connections can bump or scrape skin. Move slowly and watch the person's legs.

All the parts

Know the parts: frame, seat, wheels, brakes, leg rests and foot plates.

6 Juan and the weight bearing hoist

Meet Juan. You will help him all through Lesson 5. Juan has only the two smallest fingers working on his left hand, and his legs are weak, so he struggles to stand alone. With the physiotherapist, he chose a weight bearing (standing) hoist to move from the bed to his motorised wheelchair.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S6 P1Before you start: read his plan, check the hoist and sling (clean, no damage, battery charged, the right size sling), set up the space, and explain each step and get his consent.
  2. NP9 S6 P2Sitting up: help Juan sit on the edge of the bed, feet flat on the floor. Use a slide sheet if he needs help to move.
  3. NP9 S6 P3The sling: a standing (transfer) sling. It goes around his lower back, under his arms, with the straps coming forward. Check it sits flat and not too high or too low.
  4. NP9 S6 P4Attach: bring the hoist close with the legs open, put his feet on the foot plate and his shins against the knee pad, and clip the sling straps onto the hoist arms. Check both sides.
  5. NP9 S6 P5Hands: Juan holds the hoist handles (use his right hand, and his left hand only as he is able).
  6. NP9 S6 P6Stand: tell him what is happening and raise the hoist slowly. Juan takes weight through his legs as the hoist helps him up. Watch his face and ask how he feels.
  7. NP9 S6 P7Move: move the hoist slowly to the wheelchair (brakes on). Never leave him alone in the hoist.
  8. NP9 S6 P8Lower and finish: lower him slowly onto the seat, unclip the straps, remove the sling, and move the hoist away. Make him comfortable, then wash your hands.

7 Getting into a car

Your assessment asks for the steps to help a person sit in a car. The person does as much as they can. You prompt, and help only where needed.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S7 P1Open the car door. Move the seat back if you can.
  2. NP9 S7 P2Prompt the person to stand in the doorway and hold the window ledge (or a detachable car handle) for support.
  3. NP9 S7 P3Prompt them to turn so their back is to the car door opening.
  4. NP9 S7 P4Prompt them to lean forward, lower their head, and lower their bottom onto the seat.
  5. NP9 S7 P5Encourage them to lift the right leg into the car, then the left leg.
  6. NP9 S7 P6Adjust the seat so they are comfortable. Help with the seat belt, then close the door carefully.
  7. NP9 S7 P7Help where needed at every step.

8 Bill has fallen: what do you do?

Bill has fallen in the garden at the Respite Centre. He has pain on his right side from hip to knee. Your assessment asks what you must do to get him help and get him off the floor.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S8 P1Do not lift him. He may have a broken hip. Moving him could make it much worse.
  2. NP9 S8 P2Stay with Bill, keep him calm and warm, and call for help. You have a duty of care.
  3. NP9 S8 P3A registered nurse (RN) checks him for injuries first, if one is working. If not, or if he is badly hurt, call an ambulance (000).
  4. NP9 S8 P4Only after he has been checked, and if it is safe, a non-weight bearing (full body) hoist is used to lift him off the floor into a chair or bed.
  5. NP9 S8 P5At home or in the community, the ambulance is the first call. Paramedics can check him and treat any injuries.
  6. NP9 S8 P6Afterwards: write an incident report and tell your supervisor.

9 Mobility aids: follow the plan

Teagan's plan says she uses a hopper frame to walk. A wheelie walker is free in the corridor. Why can you not give it to her?

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S9 P1You do not have the specialist knowledge to assess her mobility and choose a walking aid. A physiotherapist does that.
  2. NP9 S9 P2You are only covered (insurance, your job description) to follow the individual plan. If you work outside your scope and she is hurt, you may lose the support of your organisation and union.
  3. NP9 S9 P3The wrong aid could make her fall and be injured, and you could be responsible.
  4. NP9 S9 P4If you think she needs a different aid, talk to your supervisor. Do not change it yourself.

10 Pressure injuries: how they happen, the stages, and prevention

A pressure injury (also called a pressure sore, bed sore or pressure ulcer) is damage to the skin and the tissue under it. It happens when a person sits or lies in one position too long: the blood cannot get through, and the tissue starts to die. The two causes are pressure and shear.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP9 S10 P1Where: skin over bony areas: the back of the head, shoulders, back, elbows, buttocks (sacrum), hips, ankles and heels.
  2. NP9 S10 P2Who is at risk: older people, people who cannot move themselves, people in bed or a wheelchair most of the day, people who are incontinent, thin, dehydrated or in pain.
  3. NP9 S10 P3Look and report: check the skin every time you help with personal care. A red area that stays red when you press it is the first warning. Report it straight away and write it down.
  4. NP9 S10 P4Move the person often: help them change position regularly (about every two hours if they cannot move themselves), or as the plan says. Encourage them to move themselves if they can.
  5. NP9 S10 P5Move without dragging: use slide sheets and hoists to avoid friction and shear. Lift heels off the bed with pillows.
  6. NP9 S10 P6Skin care: keep skin clean and dry, manage incontinence quickly, use gentle cleanser and moisturiser, do not rub or massage red areas, use barrier cream as the plan says.
  7. NP9 S10 P7Equipment (assistive technology for pressure area management): pressure relieving mattresses and cushions, heel protectors, a monkey bar so the person can shift themselves.
  8. NP9 S10 P8Food and drink: enough water, and food with protein helps the skin stay healthy.
  9. NP9 S10 P9Pressure injuries are a workplace risk. If you notice one, you report it so the team can act fast.

Stage 1

The skin is not broken. It is red (or darker on dark skin) and stays red when you press it. It may feel warm, firm, sore or itchy.

Stage 2

The top layer of skin is broken: a shallow open wound or a blister (clear or yellow fluid). It hurts.

Stage 3

The wound goes through the skin into the fat underneath. It looks like a crater, and can go in under the edges of the skin. It may smell and get infected.

Stage 4

The deepest stage: muscle, tendon or even bone can be seen. Very painful, high risk of infection. The person needs hospital care.

Unstageable

The bottom of the wound is covered with yellow, green or brown dead tissue (slough) or a black, hard crust (eschar), so nobody can see how deep it is.
