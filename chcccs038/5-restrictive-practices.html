1. Restrictive practices are actions and policies used to control or limit the actions/behaviour of people. While they can be meant for safety, they have been misused to control and deny rights. Sometimes, people need higher support levels. Laws and standards guide organizations to make sure that when these practices are used:

  • They're genuinely needed.
  • They are the least controlling option.
  • The person is supported throughout the process.

Using regulated restrictive practices is guided by the human rights framework because:

  • Using restrictive practices goes against human rights.
  • Restrictive practices take away a person's right to make choices for themselves.

2. *CHCCCS038* Using unregulated restrictive practices in the past had negative effects on the person. If these practices continue, the person is at risk of physical, psychological, and emotional problems. Here are some risks if unregulated restrictive practices persist:

  • Trauma and re-traumatisation.
  • Poor health.
  • Reduced lifespan and possibly death.
  • Feeling treated cruelly.
  • Feeling treated inhumanely.
  • Feeling denied basic rights.
  • Feeling rights and freedoms trivialised.
  • Feeling dehumanised, degraded, and disrespected.

3. Unauthorised restrictive practices

  • Basket hold: Holding a person by wrapping your arm/s around their upper and/or lower body.
  • Punitive approaches: Using threats or making someone take cold baths on purpose.
  • Overcorrection: For example, if a child draws on their desk, they might be made to clean the whole classroom.
  • Denial of key needs: Not giving someone the support they need, like keeping their belongings or seeing their family.
  • Practices related to degradation or vilification: Doing things that make the person feel degraded or demeaned.
  • Practices that limit or deny access to culture: Limiting opportunities to be part of the community, culture, or language.
  • Response cost: Making a person stop doing something because of their behavior.

4. Unauthorised restrictive practices, or authorised restrictive practices when they are not necessary should be reported to management, because inappropriate use of restrictive practices are reportable under SIRS (in aged care) and are deemed a Reportable Incident under NDIS. *CHCCCS038*

5. Types of Restrictive Practices with examples

*CHCCCS038*

Chemical: Using medication to sedate someone, especially when they don't need it to sleeep, or giving them drugs meant for mental conditions they don't have

Example 1: Travis is on a new medication. Benzodiazepines are used to treat anxiety and insomnia, by subduing a person’s nervous system and motor function. They can assist a person to relax their muscles and mind, making them feel calm and sleepy. Travis does not experience anxiety or insomnia.

Example 2: Betty gets a little more confused late in the afternoon. Staff see her walking down the corridors, opening doors, and calling out “COOEEE”. She has stated that she’s looking for her children, and that it is time for their dinner. Staff have asked that she has some extra medication to ‘calm her down’.

Mechanical: Restricting a person's movement by using chairs that recline back, have high sides, or slide in tables, where the person is unable to get out, bed rails, or splints on a arm that stop them from moving or bend it.

Example1: At breakfast, Jodi sits in a chair that has an attached tray table. Staff don’t remove the tray table from the chair until after Jodi has finished morning tea.

Physical: Physically restraining a person, like tying them to a bed or using restrictive clothing, belts, straps or physically holding onto a person to stop their movement

Example 1: When showering, if one staff member pins Maggie’s hands down, it enables the other staff member to get her washed and dried without Maggie interfering.

Example 2 :Huang is unsettled some nights, finding it difficult to sleep. The nights where he doesn’t sleep, staff transfer him to a mobile recliner and apply a posy vest. This way Huang won’t be able to walk around and disturb others who are sleeping.

Environmental: Keeping someone locked in, high fences with no gates or blocking their access to things they need, like food in a locked fridge or putting stuff out of their reach.

Example: Jason moved into a new two storey home last month. His parents have said that he needs to wait until after the lounge room has been refurbished, before they build the external ramp to the front door. Jason lives alone and requires a wheelchair for all mobility

Example 2:Leeza fears that her husband will get hurt if he goes outside without her, so she has put a padlock on the backdoor.

Seclusion: Keeping someone alone in a room, is not assisted to exit their room or interact with others, often with the door locked from the outside.

Example1 : Wazza is extra careful to always do what he is told to, because if he does not follow all of Bea’s orders when they are in town, he has to spend an hour in his room when they get home.

Example 2 : Since Henry’s dementia has progressed, his wife has cancelled the weekly golf morning Henry had with his friends, and his mobile phone service.

6. Use of authorised restrictive practices as a last resort and proportionate to the risk of potential harm to the person or others

Before regulated restrictive practices can be used, the following must be taken into consideration by the person applying for the authority to use restrictive practices.

  • Are we safeguarding rights?
  • Is it the least restrictive way to manage the behaviour of harm?
  • Does a practice adhere to relevant legislation, conventions policies and procedures?
  • Is the practice evidence based (this may require consultation)?
  • Have the pros and cons been considered?
  • Is restrictive practice still considered the best approach in the short term?

The procedure for using restrictive practices must be clearly outlined as well as a reduction strategy - the restrictive practices that may be used after the considerations above were made and it is deemed to be the last resort available to manage the behaviour.

7. NDIS guidelines require organisations to report all instances where restrictive practices have been used, to the Commission. It is important to note that staff will not be subject to any repercussions for reporting use of restrictive practices.

Organisations providing support to the person with a disability must have policies and procedures in place about the use of regulated restrictive practices.

8. Limited Restrictive Practices may be employed in situations where a Behaviour support plan (aged care) / Positive behaviour support plan (disability) has been developed, and the use has been authorised as a last resort.

*CHCCCS038*

9. A Behaviour Support Plan is like a detailed guide that helps manage changes in a person's behaviour. The main aim is to encourage positive behaviour and improve the person's well-being and quality of life.

To create this plan, a team completes assessments, gathers information from the person's interactions (including things that might trigger behaviour changes), and notes which strategies have worked or not. If any restrictive practices are considered, there must be proof that the person or someone authorized by them has agreed to use them.

This plan is put together by the organization's management, with input from health professionals and the person or their authorized decision maker. In the Disability sector, a similar process is followed, but for the use of Restrictive Practices, the organization needs approval from QCAT (Queensland Civil and Administrative Tribunal). If there's an urgent need, short-term requests can be made to the Public Guardian or the Chief Executive of the Department.

10. A positive behaviour support plan (PBSP) must be written that includes positive strategies to be attempted before using a restrictive practice.

11. A positive behavior support plan helps people with input from their family, carer, or guardian. It empowers the person by letting them suggest better ways to deal with their behavior before using extreme measures. The goal of the plan is to:

  • Respect the person and improve their life.
  • Use current evidence-based methods.
  • Reduce harmful behaviors.
  • Decrease and stop using extreme measures.
  • Create custom strategies that fit the person's needs, to reduce troubling behaviors and avoid extreme measures.
  • Focus on the person's needs to address the reasons behind the challenging behaviors.

12. When restrictive practices are needed, the documentation must include:

  • Why it's the least restrictive way to keep the person or others safe.
  • The steps to use it.
  • Other ways to care for and keep the person safe.
  • How it affects the person, both positively and negatively.
  • How often it will be checked.
  • Any approvals and permissions need to be reported to the Queensland government, no matter what type they are.

13. Positive strategies, like behaviour support plans, are crucial for reducing restrictive practices. They:

  • Gather important info about a person's background and behaviour.
  • Help providers recognize when changes are needed to meet the person's needs.
  • Deal with the root causes of concerns, keeping the person safe and well.
  • Use assessments to understand the behaviours and what triggers them.
  • Offer details about different strategies (whether they worked or not) used to handle these behaviours.
