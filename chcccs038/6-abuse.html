A. Abuse Def - External agencies and complaints

A. Abuse is any activity within a relationship of trust that results in harm to an older or disabled person’.

Unfortunately there may be times when you are concerned that a person is being subjected to some form of abuse.

What to do? - Even if you do not have clear evidence of abuse occurring, it is important that these concerns are reported immediately to your supervisor and document according to organisational polidy. All indicators of abuse should be reported to supervisor/management with 24 hours

External agencies that can provide support:

  • Advocacy service,
  • elder abuse prevention unit,
  • counselor

For complaints

  • Aged care - Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission
  • Disability - NDIS Commission

C1. Types of abuse and Signs

*CHCCCS038* C.1

Verbal abuse: This is when someone uses loud, threatening, or intimidating language. Signs may include the person becoming withdrawn, nervous, or hesitant to interact with others.

Physical abuse: This involves physical harm like hitting, slapping, or restraining. Signs can include bruises, bite marks, fractures, frequent visits to hospitals, or a person appearing nervous, withdrawing from contact, or wearing concealing clothing.

Sexual abuse: This includes indecent exposure, harassment, rape, or unwanted sexual touching. Signs may involve the person having sexually transmitted diseases, bleeding, or being reluctant to engage in physical contact. They might also appear nervous, emotional, or withdrawn.

Financial abuse: This occurs when someone takes money or property without permission. Signs may include the person struggling to pay bills, feeling distressed due to a lack of funds, or losing access to their financial documents.

Emotional/Social abuse: This prevents a person from socializing or making friends. Signs may involve the person avoiding activities, social events, or community gatherings, and they might frequently appear tearful or emotional.

Intentional Neglect: This is when someone intentionally fails to provide essential care, like medications or food. Signs could include the person losing weight, developing pressure wounds, appearing unkempt, or experiencing repeated illnesses and infections.

Unintentional Neglect: This happens when a caregiver cannot provide care due to illness or learning difficulties. Signs may be similar to intentional neglect, such as weight loss, broken glasses or dentures, or frequent illnesses.

Psychological abuse: Psychological abuse involves tactics such as manipulating, threatening, or isolating a person emotionally. Signs may include a person becoming excessively anxious, displaying low self-esteem, appearing fearful, or becoming withdrawn and emotionally fragile.

Systemic abuse happens when there are problems in how support is given. Examples can be a person not wanting help from certain staff, not having enough resources to meet their needs, getting the support they agreed on, but there's no plan made for them, or when complaints are ignored.

C.2 - (CHCCCS033) - Types of Abuse - Indicators - Behaviours of the abuser (perpetrator)

Financial Abuse

'The improper use of another person's assets or the use or withholding of another person's resources.'

Signs/Indicators

  • Changes in behaviour when around a specific person.
  • Missing personal items with unclear explanations.
  • Unpaid bills, despite the ability to pay.
  • Less money in the bank than usual.
  • Not enough money for essentials like food and clothing.
  • An empty fridge or lack of food at home.
  • may include the person struggling to pay bills, feeling distressed due to a lack of funds, or losing access to their financial documents.

Behaviours of the abuser (perpetrator)

  • Threaten the victim to get money or access to accounts.
  • Steal personal items.
  • Take over their bank account.
  • Block access to their own money.
  • Misuse powers of attorney.
  • Alter their will.

Exploitation

'The illegal, unauthorized, or improper use of a person's resources by a caregiver or other person in a trusting relationship, for the benefit of someone other than the person you support.'

Indicators / Signs

  • Selling or leasing their home or car without a clear reason.
  • Losing access to their bank account.
  • Letting others live in their home without helping with costs.
  • Sending money to strangers online.
  • Having little or no food at home.

A person exploiting someone may:

  • Sell their belongings.
  • Use a fake friendship or romance to take their money.
  • Block access to their bank account or belongings.
  • Misuse power of attorney rights

Undue influence

'Undue influence refers to a situation where the weaker party is influenced into agreeing. Undue influence can be 'actual' or 'presumed.' Actual undue influence may arise because of physical coercion which prevents the exercise of independent judgment.'

Signs of undue influence include:

  • The person’s original intentions for their will are overturned.

An abuser may:

  • Change the will after the person can no longer make decisions.
  • Pressure the person to change the will in their favour.
  • Alter the will to benefit themselves.

Emotional and psychological

'Verbal assaults, threats of maltreatment, harassment, humiliation or intimidation, or failure to interact with a person or to acknowledge that person's existence.'

Signs of emotional or psychological abuse:

  • Changes in behavior, like being tearful, confused, or agitated.
  • Saying negative things like "nothing good ever happens to me."
  • Showing depression, hopelessness, or fear.
  • Acting ashamed or helpless.
  • Talking about being watched or harmed.
  • Changes in sleep, unusual behavior, or loss of interest in activities.
  • Less interaction with others and lower self-esteem.
  • Acting differently after seeing certain people.

Behaviors of the abuser:

  • Calling them cruel names or treating them like a child.
  • Making threats to harm them or their pets.
  • Using blackmail, like limiting contact with loved ones.
  • Blocking access to phones, mail, or social connections.
  • Preventing participation in cultural, spiritual, or religious events.
  • Isolating them by moving them away from family and friends.

Physical

Any non-accidental physical injury or injuries to a person or adult.

Signs of physical abuse:

  • Unexplained injuries, such as: Sprains, bruises, cuts, or burns. Fractures, dislocations, or internal injuries. Healing wounds or broken bones. Missing or broken teeth. Black eyes, welts, or slap marks. Rope or strap marks, and cigarette burns. Burn or scald marks.
  • Frequent unexplained doctor or hospital visits.

Behaviors of the abuser:

  • Rough or harmful actions, like: Shoving, hitting, punching, or slapping. Kicking, biting, stabbing, or burning. Restraining with ropes, locking them in, or tying them down. Using objects or weapons to harm. Overusing or misusing medication.

Sexual

'Any sexual contact between an adult or person 16 years of age and younger; or any sexual activity with an adult who is unable to understand, has not given consent, is threatened, coerced or forced to engage in sexual behaviour.'

Signs of sexual abuse:

The person may report:

  • Being touched in a way that made them uncomfortable or scared.
  • Someone having sex with them without consent.

You may observe:

  • Nakedness or torn clothing.
  • Bruising or blood near the mouth, genitals, breasts, or rectum.
  • Unexplained sexually transmitted infections.
  • Behavior changes like trouble sleeping, sleeping fully dressed, avoiding usual places, or being overly compliant.
  • Inappropriate sexual knowledge for their age or background.

Behaviors of the abuser:

  • Making sexual comments or touching without consent.
  • Touching private areas like the breasts, genitals, or rectum.
  • Tearing or removing clothing or leaving the person naked.
  • Offering gifts in exchange for sex.

Coercion

'Specifically, coercion and manipulation may involve physical and emotional trickery, flattery, bribery and intimidation, threats of harm or loss of power or safety. Coercion and manipulation involve a power imbalance between the alleged abuser and the child.'

Signs of coercion:

A victim may:

  • Feel "special" due to receiving gifts others don’t get.
  • Avoid socializing and prefer to be alone.
  • Keep secrets or talk less with family and friends.
  • Show fear, anxiety, low self-esteem, or confusion about healthy relationships.
  • Display signs of distress.

Behaviors of the abuser:

  • Spend extra time with the victim or give them gifts.
  • Offer support and claim to deeply care for them.
  • Favour the victim over others in the family.
  • Build trust with the family by offering emotional, financial, or practical support.
  • Suggest the victim is lying if they speak out.
  • Attempt to form a sexual relationship with the victim.

Grooming

'Grooming refers to actions deliberately undertaken with the aim of engaging and influencing a child, employee, or in some circumstances members of the child's family, for the purpose of sexual activity with a child.'

Signs of Grooming

A victim, often under 16, may:

  • Have family problems or low self-esteem.
  • Spend a lot of time alone, online, or gaming.
  • Isolate from family and friends and become dependent on the abuser.
  • Believe the abuser is a good friend who listens and helps.
  • Struggle with eating, sleeping, trusting others, or forming healthy relationships.

Behaviors of Groomers

People who groom others may:

  • Spend time in online chat sites and communities.
  • Be a family friend or someone close to the victim's family.
  • Pretend to be the same age and share similar interests online.
  • Use social media to learn about the victim’s life to form a connection.
  • Befriend the victim and share secrets to gain trust.
  • Offer gifts or money for nude photos or online sexual activities.
  • Invite the victim to parties with drugs or alcohol.
  • Sexualize the relationship by asking or encouraging sexual questions or photos.

Neglect

'Any act or failure to act that results in a breach of a person's human rights, civil liberties, physical and mental integrity, dignity or general well-being, whether intended or through negligence.'

Signs of Neglect

  • Wearing clothes that aren’t suitable for the weather, are dirty, or damaged.
  • Not having enough food or clothing.
  • Not receiving proper care, like dental or medical treatment, or not getting medications.
  • Injuries not treated, or missing support aids (e.g., wheelchairs, glasses).
  • Living in an unsafe or uncomfortable environment (too hot/cold).
  • Unexplained weight loss, dehydration, or poor skin health.

Behaviors of the Neglector

  • Failing to provide food, clothing, or medications.
  • Leaving the person outside or in dangerous situations.
  • Not giving medicines as needed or providing proper supervision.
  • Leaving the person alone for long periods or being overly attentive when others are around.
  • Denying others the chance to help or leaving the person in extreme temperatures.

Systemic abuse

'Failure to recognise, provide or attempt to provide adequate or appropriate services, including services that are appropriate to that person's age, gender, culture, needs or preferences.'

Signs of Systemic Abuse

  • Living in an environment where rules don’t meet individual needs, like using incontinence pads when able to use the toilet.
  • Being physically restrained to prevent falls.
  • Not being able to attend programs or activities because they:
    • Are not interesting or too complicated.
    • Lack transport or aren’t in a language the person understands.
    • Have negative experiences with staff in the past.

Behaviors of Perpetrators

Management may:

  • Not train staff to recognize or handle abuse
  • .Encourage unnecessary use of incontinence pads for convenience.
  • Fail to provide same-gender staff when requested.
  • Offer unsuitable or inadequate programs.
  • Allow physical restraints due to a lack of staff.
  • Not train staff on duty of care or the code of conduct.

D2. Human Rights

D.2 - Human rights are basic rights and freedoms everyone has, including the right to be free from exploitation, violence, and abuse. When someone is a victim of these, their human rights are violated.

People with cognitive impairments may face abuse or neglect that goes unnoticed because they might not recognize or report it.

To help protect their rights:

  • Look out for signs of abuse when supporting them.
  • Attend training at your workplace on recognizing abuse and follow your organization’s reporting and documentation procedures.

D.3 - Organisational Responsibilities

To protect people from abuse, organizations must:

  • Have abuse prevention policies and procedures.
  • Provide training for staff on how to prevent abuse.
  • Use systems like:Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS) for aged care.NDIS Participant Safeguarding Policy for disability care.

Staff responsibilities:

  • Follow the abuse policies and procedures.
  • Attend training on abuse prevention.

D.4 - Consent to Report

If a person doesn’t give consent to report abuse, their decision might be overridden in certain cases, such as:

  • if the abuse is sexual or physical
  • if the abuse falls within the reportable incidents identified by the Serious Incident Response Scheme (SIRS)
  • if the abuse falls within the reportable incidents identified by NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission.

For all other types of abuse, consent is respected.

If abuse happens due to organisational processes or staff in aged care, home care, or disability services, it is recorded under the SIRS or Reportable Incidents frameworks.

D.5 - Mandatory reporting

D.5 - What is mandatory reporting?

Mandatory reporting is reporting to government agencies or commissions that is compulsory due to laws or regulations

Compulsory reported incidents

Some incidents must be reported under SIRS or NDIS Quality Standards:

Aged Care – SIRS:

  • Use of force or unlawful sexual contact.
  • Emotional or psychological abuse.
  • Neglect or unexpected death.
  • Financial abuse by staff.
  • Inappropriate use of restraints.
  • Missing residents.

Disability Sector – Reportable Incidents:

  • Sexual misconduct, grooming, or assault.
  • Physical abuse or neglect.
  • Serious injury or death.
  • Misuse of restrictive practices.

For NDIS participants, decisions putting them at high risk (e.g., serious harm, injury, or legal issues) may lead to intervention by the NDIA to protect their safety and others.

Example: If there’s a risk of death or serious harm, NDIA staff must contact emergency services or request a police welfare check, even if the person disagrees.

D.6 - Informed Consent and Actions

If the person has capacity and gives consent:

  1. Document their consent.
  2. Discuss safety and intervention options.
  3. (Implement intervention) Take action on agreed safety measures.
  4. Refer to support agencies or contacts.
  5. Advocate for them.
  6. Document all conversations and observations.
  7. Report to your supervisor and complete a report form.

If the person has capacity but does NOT consent:

  1. Document their refusal.
  2. Offer information about safety and support options.
  3. Document conversations and observations as per supervisor's advice.
  4. Report conversation and observation to your supervisor.

If the person lacks capacity and a substitute decision-maker gives consent:

  1. Document the consent.
  2. Discuss safety and intervention options.
  3. (Implement intervention)Take agreed actions for safety.
  4. Refer to support agencies.
  5. Advocate for the person.
  6. Document all conversations and observations.
  7. Report to your supervisor and complete the required form.

If no substitute decision-maker exists, contact the adult guardian for guidance.

D.10 - Dignity of Risk and Legal Protections

People have the right to take risks (dignity of risk) when making decisions. Organisations must have processes to manage and document these risks to protect both the person and themselves.

Steps to support dignity of risk:

  1. Discuss possible risks with the person or their substitute decision-maker (if needed).
  2. Identify risks and plan strategies to manage them.
  3. Use a Dignity of Risk form to record:Details of the risks.Confirmation that the person or their decision-maker understands and accepts the risks.Their signature.
  4. Ensure all documentation is completed before the activity begins.

The Dignity of Risk form also helps reduce the organisation’s legal responsibility if something goes wrong.

D.11 - Understanding Duty of Care

Duty of care means taking reasonable steps to prevent harm. If negligence occurs, a person can sue for damages.

Duty of Care in Abuse Situations:

  • It is breached if the person is placed back with their abuser without a court order.
  • It is breached if staff fail to act to prevent harm or ignore abuse.
  • Duty of care applies if you are paid to care for the person.

How to Maintain Duty of Care:

  1. Confirm if your organisation has a contract to provide care—if yes, duty of care applies.
  2. Take all actions within your role to prevent abuse.
  3. Follow your organisation's abuse policies and procedures.

Examples of Breaching Duty of Care:

  • Placing a person back with their abuser.
  • Ignoring abuse and failing to prevent harm.
  • Failing to act when abuse was foreseeable.
  • Not informing the person about their rights.

D.12 - Code of Conduct for Support Workers

Support workers must follow their organisation's Code of Conduct to ensure the wellbeing and safety of the people they support.

All organisations will have a document for staff to read called 'Code of Conduct' which provides all staff members with information about how to act in certain situations. The Code of Conduct document clearly outlines your responsibilities to ensure you provide care, support, and services free from all forms of violence, neglect, abuse, and sexual misconduct.

What to Do if You See a Problem:

  • Take immediate action to keep the person safe.
  • Follow the organisation’s policies, procedures, and codes of conduct.

What the Code of Conduct Covers:

The Code of Conduct explains:

  • How to act in different situations.
  • Your responsibility to provide care free from violence, neglect, abuse, or misconduct.

E - Roles, responsibilities and responding to claims and incidents

E.1 - Supporting the person

E.1 - Supporting the person

Prevention of abuse is the best way to keep the people you support safe.

Part of your role and responsibility to keep the person you support safe is to be aware of the indicators of abuse, look for abuse indicators and report indicators of abuse. If abuse has occurred, you must support the person and follow reporting requirements.

Abuse Policy and Procedures (DOC 53 KB)

Abuse Policy - Connect

Process

Step 1:

Support the person

  • Listen to the person's story.
  • Acknowledge what they are saying to you.
  • Validate their experience by letting them know you believe them.

Step 2:

Assess the risk

  • Determine the level and urgency of safety concerns.
  • Call your supervisor.
  • If the person is at risk of further abuse call emergency services - police and ambulance.
  • Do not leave the person in an abusive situation if it is safe for you to get them away.

Step 3:

Complete the correct documentation

This will vary depending on:

  • Whether the abuse is actual or suspected
  • Where the abuse has occurred
  • Who the perpetrator of the abuse is.

If you work as an independent NDIS support worker, the National Disability Insurance Agency (NDIA) will guide you through the process.

E.2 - Before you can start reassuring the person you will need to assess their wellbeing.

E.2

E.2

Before you can start reassuring the person you will need to assess their wellbeing.

If the person has not had to travel for medical assistance via the ambulance to hospital, you may be the first or only staff member supporting the person before your supervisor, or a support person arrives.

Check and support their wellbeing before asking questions about the abuse.

  • Do you have pain?
  • Can you move?
  • Do you need assistance to do an activity? (e.g. walk, sit down or go back to bed)
  • Would you like a drink of water, tea or coffee?

Once the person is settled, they may be ready to discuss the incident.

This is when your listening skills need to be turned on.

When the person is ready to talk about the abuse incident, your active listening skills will be required to ensure the person doesn’t have to repeat things unnecessarily, to encourage them to continue talking and to ensure you clearly remember what the person has said.

Listening skills include verbal and nonverbal communication.

Nodding and tilting your head.

Sit next the person instead of in front of them.

Make soft eye contact.

Keep your arms at your side with your palms open.

  • Repeating – repeating the message using exactly the same words as the person
  • Paraphrasing - repeating the message using similiar words and phrase arrangement
  • Reflecting - repeating the message using your own words
  • Responses such as ‘Uh-Huh’, ‘Mmm’ , ‘Yes’, ‘Continue’ and ‘Tell me more’

When you are supporting the person, you will be explaining procedures, their rights regarding reporting the incident and reporting abuse protocols. During this time, the person or their support person may still be upset or have difficulty understanding what you are saying.

As part of your job role, you need to ensure the person, or their support person understands what you are saying.

  • Ask open ended questions
  • Ask the person if they understand
  • Observe if the person follows instructions
  • Ask the person to repeat back to you some of what has been said.
  • Seek feedback from the person

The person who has experienced an abusive incident needs to feel they are believed and reassured that you will do what you can to support them. This requires you to know how to use reassurance strategies. Reassurance strategies can be verbal or physical.

  • ‘I understand you are upset.’
  • ‘It is reasonable to be upset.’
  • ‘Would you like me to call someone for you?’
  • ‘Would you like a tissue?’
  • ‘Can I get you anything?’
  • ‘Yes, I believe you.’

E.3 - Safeguard strategies

  • Provide education about the person's choice if they are choosing to stay in an abusive relationship.
  • Make referrals to social workers and/or mental health workers.
  • Help the person set up a pre-set plan to stay with other family members or friends if abuse is likely to occur again. This could also include an escape plan.
  • Providing contact numbers or contact them (for example, the Elder Abuse Helpline or National Disability Abuse and Neglect Hotline) on behalf of the person.
  • Supply contact numbers for legal aid, including LGBTI legal services and Aboriginal Torres Strait Islander Partnership Legal Aid
  • Along with contact numbers for the department of seniors, and disability services, give information about services such as police and ambulance, 'Respect.' Domestic violence support and other government abuse support agencies.
  • Provide contact numbers for advocacy networks.
