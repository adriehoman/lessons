This lesson is about trust. People trust you with their private information, with their bodies and with their safety. Here you learn how to keep that trust, and what the law says you must do.

1 Privacy, confidentiality and disclosure

The Privacy Act 1988 (Cth) and the Information Privacy Act 2009 (Qld) protect personal information: anything that tells you who a person is or something about them.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S1 P1Privacy and confidentiality means you protect personal information. You do not repeat private conversations, and files are locked away.
  2. NP7 S1 P2You only share information with people who need to know to do their job, for example your supervisor or the nurse.
  3. NP7 S1 P3Disclosure is sharing personal or key information for a clear reason, with the right person. For example: you tell your supervisor that a resident said he wants to hurt himself.
  4. NP7 S1 P4Talk about people in a private place, not in the lift, the café or on social media.
  5. NP7 S1 P5Never post photos or stories about the people you support online, even without their name.
  6. NP7 S1 P6New since 2025 Since 2025 a person can take legal action for a serious invasion of their privacy, and Queensland government services must report data breaches.

2 How an organisation protects information **

Your organisation must keep personal information safe. Here are six ways it does that.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S2 P1All files are locked in a secure place (a locked cabinet in a locked room).
  2. NP7 S2 P2Computers have passwords, and every worker has their own login.
  3. NP7 S2 P3Information is not shared without the person's consent.
  4. NP7 S2 P4Security software is installed on computers.
  5. NP7 S2 P5There is a privacy policy that explains how information is collected, stored, used and shared.
  6. NP7 S2 P6Papers with personal details are shredded or put in the confidential waste bin.
  7. NP7 S2 P7Staff information is protected too. An organisation that gives staff names and addresses to a shop, so the shop can send them sales letters, breaks privacy law. It can face legal action, staff stop trusting their employer, and it can put its accreditation at risk.

3 Work practices that need fixing

Some everyday habits break legal or ethical rules. Spot them and fix them.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S3 P1Files left on a desk after hours: anyone can read them. Lock them away.
  2. NP7 S3 P2Using your phone for your own social media during work hours: you are not focused on the person, and private things can end up online.
  3. NP7 S3 P3Your children coming into the workplace after school: a risk for privacy, safety and insurance.
  4. NP7 S3 P4Taking money from a person to do your own shopping: this can be financial abuse.
  5. NP7 S3 P5These are good practices: writing progress notes after each episode of care, and using your phone to look up information for the person.

4 Informed consent **

Informed consent means the person understands and then agrees. You need it before you give support.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S4 P1Step 1: Clear information. Explain what you want to do, in words the person understands (simple words, their language, pictures or an interpreter if needed).
  2. NP7 S4 P2Step 2: Agreement. The person agrees to the support or the task. Consent is their agreement for staff to support them.
  3. NP7 S4 P3Step 3: Risks. The person knows the risks and benefits of their choice, and that they can say no or change their mind.
  4. NP7 S4 P4Why it is needed: it respects the person's rights and choices, and it protects you and the organisation. Support without consent can be assault.
  5. NP7 S4 P5Ask every time. Consent for a shower yesterday is not consent for a shower today.
  6. NP7 S4 P6Informed choice: the person decides after they have all the information and enough time to think. New since 2025 Under the new Aged Care Act, every older person is presumed able to make decisions, and can choose a registered supporter to help them decide.

5 Discrimination: what it is **

Discrimination is when a person or a group is treated less fairly than others because of their background or a personal attribute. This is called direct discrimination.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S5 P1Discrimination can be against the law when it is because of a person's age.
  2. NP7 S5 P2Disability.
  3. NP7 S5 P3Race, including colour, national or ethnic origin, or immigrant status.
  4. NP7 S5 P4Sex, pregnancy, marital or relationship status, family responsibilities or breastfeeding.
  5. NP7 S5 P5Sexual orientation, gender identity or intersex status.
  6. NP7 S5 P6In Queensland the Anti-Discrimination Act 1991 (Qld) also covers religious belief or activity, political belief, trade union activity and more.
  7. NP7 S5 P7Discrimination can happen when people apply for a job, get a service, rent a home, or try to get into a building.
Read more in Connect: Anti-discrimination Act 1991 (Qld)Disability Discrimination Act 1992 (Cth)Age Discrimination Act 2004 (Cth)

6 Discrimination at work **

Here are real examples from community services. Each one is against the law, so it is a legal concern, not only an ethical one.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S6 P1Age: Joan is an older worker. She is told she did not get the activities job because it "suits a much younger person". That is age discrimination (Age Discrimination Act 2004, Anti-Discrimination Act 1991).
  2. NP7 S6 P2Religion: staff leave Abdul out of the day outing because his prayer times are "too hard". That is discrimination because of his religion, not a choice he made.
  3. NP7 S6 P3Sex and age in a job advert: a service advertises for "male staff aged 25 to 30". The organisation breaks the anti-discrimination law, can face legal action, and its image in the community is damaged.
  4. NP7 S6 P4Disability: a person who uses a wheelchair is refused a service because the building has no ramp and no other help is offered.
  5. NP7 S6 P5Race: a person is refused a service because of their skin colour or where they come from.
  6. NP7 S6 P6Pregnancy: a worker is not given extra shifts because she is pregnant.
Watch out For Joan and Abdul, tick Legal concern, then give a reason that names the attribute and the law: "This is discrimination because of her age. It is against the Age Discrimination Act."

7 Mandatory reporting

Mandatory reporting means the law says you must report something, for example suspected abuse or neglect of a vulnerable person.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S7 P1In aged care, providers must report serious incidents (SIRS, next section).
  2. NP7 S7 P2In disability, NDIS providers must report reportable incidents to the NDIS Commission.
  3. NP7 S7 P3In Queensland, some workers (for example teachers, doctors and registered nurses) must report suspected harm to a child. Most organisations ask all their staff to report it too.
  4. NP7 S7 P4Your part: if you see or suspect abuse or neglect, make the person safe, then report to your supervisor straight away, and write down the facts.

8 SIRS in aged care **

The SIRS (Serious Incident Response Scheme) helps reduce abuse and neglect of older people in aged care.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S8 P1Aged care providers must identify, record, manage, fix and report all serious incidents, including incidents that are only suspected (alleged).
  2. NP7 S8 P2It covers people living in residential aged care homes. New since 2025 It also covers people who get aged care at home (home services).
  3. NP7 S8 P3Reports go to the Aged Care Quality and Safety Commission. The most serious (Priority 1) must be reported within 24 hours.
  4. NP7 S8 P4Reportable incidents: unreasonable use of force (hitting, pushing, rough handling).
  5. NP7 S8 P5Unlawful sexual contact or inappropriate sexual conduct.
  6. NP7 S8 P6Neglect (for example no help with meals, wounds not treated, no way to call for help).
  7. NP7 S8 P7Psychological or emotional abuse (yelling, name calling, ignoring the person).
  8. NP7 S8 P8Unexpected death.
  9. NP7 S8 P9Stealing from the person, or financial coercion by a staff member.
  10. NP7 S8 P10Inappropriate use of restrictive practices (physical or chemical restraint).
  11. NP7 S8 P11Unexplained absence from care (the person goes missing).
Watch out Your answer needs two parts: a short explanation of SIRS and two examples of incidents you must report. Many answers forget the examples.

9 NDIS reportable incidents **

In disability services, NDIS providers must tell the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission about reportable incidents that happen, or are said to happen, while giving NDIS supports.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S9 P1The death of a person with disability.
  2. NP7 S9 P2Serious injury of a person with disability.
  3. NP7 S9 P3Abuse or neglect of a person with disability.
  4. NP7 S9 P4Unlawful sexual or physical contact with, or assault of, a person with disability.
  5. NP7 S9 P5Sexual misconduct against, or in front of, a person with disability.
  6. NP7 S9 P6Using a restrictive practice that is not written and authorised in the person's behaviour support plan.
  7. NP7 S9 P7Most must be reported within 24 hours.
Read more in Connect: Mandatory and organisational reportingNational Disability Insurance Scheme Act 2013 (Cth)

10 Restrictive practices **

A restrictive practice is anything that limits a person's rights or freedom to move. It is only allowed in a few cases, and it is strictly controlled by law.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP7 S10 P1Physical restraint: holding a person so they cannot move.
  2. NP7 S10 P2Chemical restraint: giving medicine to control behaviour (not to treat an illness).
  3. NP7 S10 P3Mechanical restraint: using a device to stop movement, for example a lap belt or bed rails that are not in the plan.
  4. NP7 S10 P4Environmental restraint: limiting what a person can reach or use, for example taking away their call bell or walking frame, or locking the kitchen.
  5. NP7 S10 P5Seclusion: keeping a person alone in a room they cannot leave.
  6. NP7 S10 P6A restrictive practice must be a last resort, written in the person's plan, agreed to (consent), and checked often. If it is used without this, it can be neglect or abuse, and it must be reported.
