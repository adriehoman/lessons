1 Who aged care is for

Aged care is help for older people who need support with everyday living.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S1 P1Aged care helps older people with everyday living. The help can be in their own home, for a short stay, or in an aged care home.
  2. NP4 S1 P2In Australia, aged care is for people aged 65 and over. For Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people it starts at 50 and over.
  3. NP4 S1 P3The number of Australians aged 85 and over is growing fast: from about 515,700 in 2019 to more than 1.5 million by 2058. Many of them are frail and have more than one health condition.
  4. NP4 S1 P4More older people means more jobs for support workers, at home and in aged care homes. You can also study further later, for example to become a nurse.
  5. NP4 S1 P5Older people have lived long lives. You support them, and you can also learn from their stories and experience.

2 Three main types of care **

Older people can get care in three main ways. The right one depends on what the person needs and wants.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S2 P1Residential care: the person lives in an aged care home. Staff are there 24 hours a day.
  2. NP4 S2 P2In-home care (home care): the person stays in their own home. Workers visit to help, for example with showering, cleaning, shopping or getting to appointments.
  3. NP4 S2 P3Respite care: short-term care. It gives the family carer a break, or helps the person recover, for example after a fall or a hospital stay. It can be in an aged care home, a day centre or a cottage.

3 How aged care is paid for **

Aged care costs a lot of money. Most of it comes from the Australian Government. The person pays part if they can.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S3 P1The Australian Government pays aged care providers in three ways: subsidies and supplements, capital grants (money for buildings and equipment), and program funding.
  2. NP4 S3 P2The person also pays part of the cost through fees and charges, if they can afford it.
  3. NP4 S3 P3Aged care homes: AN-ACC. Since October 2022, an independent assessor checks each new resident and gives them an AN-ACC class. The class decides part of the money the home gets to care for that person.
  4. NP4 S3 P4Care at home: CHSP or HCP. First an ACAT assessment finds out how much help the person needs. Then the government pays for that help through the Commonwealth Home Support Program (CHSP) or a Home Care Package (HCP). Being replaced
  5. NP4 S3 P5New since 2025 On 1 November 2025, Support at Home replaced Home Care Packages. CHSP will join Support at Home from July 2027 or later. Since December 2024 the Single Assessment System does the assessments that ACAT did. Your learning resources and your assessment still use CHSP, HCP and ACAT, so learn those words.

4 Words you will hear every day **

Aged care uses a lot of short forms. When you write one, give the full words first, then say what it means.

Listen to the explanation

You will meet two more short forms, IMS and SIRS, in Lesson 4 when we learn about keeping people safe.

ADLs: Activities of Daily Living

The everyday tasks a person needs to do: showering, dressing, toileting, eating and getting around. You help with ADLs every shift.

ACAT: Aged Care Assessment Team

A team of health workers who assess an older person's needs and decide what government-funded care they can get.

HCP: Home Care Package

Government-funded help at home with four levels: level 1 is low care and level 4 is the highest care. Being replaced

CHSP: Commonwealth Home Support Program

Government-funded low-level help for people living in their own homes, like cleaning, meals or transport.

AN-ACC: Australian National Aged Care Classification

The way aged care homes are funded. An independent assessor gives each resident a class, and the class decides part of the funding.

5 Big issues in aged care today **

The Royal Commission into Aged Care Quality and Safety (final report 2021) looked closely at aged care in Australia. It found some big problems.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S5 P1Changing demographics: more people live longer, many are frail, and most want to stay in their own home as long as they can. They also expect more choice.
  2. NP4 S5 P2Workload: more older people to support means more work for each staff member.
  3. NP4 S5 P3An ageing workforce: many workers are older themselves. The sector needs to attract younger workers.
  4. NP4 S5 P4Staff shortages and funding: there is not always enough money for enough staff to meet everyone's needs.
  5. NP4 S5 P5Technology: assistive technology can help people stay independent, when services use it well.
  6. NP4 S5 P6Access: many older people find the aged care system hard to understand and hard to get into.
  7. NP4 S5 P7Substandard care and abuse: the Royal Commission found care that was not good enough, and cases of abuse.

The Royal Commission asked for a new law that puts the rights of older people first. That law is the Aged Care Act 2024. You will learn about it in Lesson 3.

6 The old way: grouping older people apart **

In the past, many older people were moved into large institutions, away from younger people. This was meant to stop loneliness. It caused new problems.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S6 P1It can lead to ageism and stereotyping: people see "the old people" as a group, not as individuals.
  2. NP4 S6 P2It separates the generations. Young people miss the experience and care of older people. Older people miss the energy of the young, and they become lonely.
  3. NP4 S6 P3People of different ages miss chances to support each other.
  4. NP4 S6 P4Older people may feel their life is empty and has no meaning.
  5. NP4 S6 P5It causes disconnection and division between age groups.

7 New ways of living **

Today aged care puts the person at the centre (person-centred care) and keeps them connected to the community. Here are some new (contemporary) ways of living.

Listen to the explanation
  1. NP4 S7 P1Small-scale domestic living: a small, home-like house. People cook, clean and live as they did in their own home.
  2. NP4 S7 P2Dementia villages: houses in a safe village, with shops and cafes that are run by village staff.
  3. NP4 S7 P3Shared housing: 6 to 10 people share a home, with a shared kitchen and living room, and at least one community care service supports them.
  4. NP4 S7 P4Teaching and research aged care services: aged care and teaching (for example, of nurses) happen in the same place.
  5. NP4 S7 P5Social and care farming: people take part in normal farm work, which is good for their physical and mental health.
  6. NP4 S7 P6Cottage and home-like respite: short stays in a purpose-built cottage instead of a big building.
  7. NP4 S7 P7Intergenerational models: older people live close to everyone else and mix with all ages, for example an aged care home next to a school, where children and residents do art, music and cooking together.
  8. NP4 S7 P8Bidyadanga dementia program: helps Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people with dementia stay living in their community, on Country.
Watch out When you describe a new way of living, name one model and explain how it works. Do not describe the old way of putting everyone in one big building.
